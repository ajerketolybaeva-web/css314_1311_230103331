
## Phase 2 — Strong Scaling and Weak Scaling

### Experimental setup

The experiment was performed using a workload with:
- Strong scaling: N = 1,600,000
- Weak scaling: N0 = 250,000 per process
- Number of processes: 1–8
- Stage A: serial computation over the first 5% of elements
- Stage B: parallel computation using a multiprocessing Pool

### Experiment 2A — Strong Scaling

| Processes | Time (ms) | Speedup | Efficiency |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 3935.057 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 |
| 2 | 2032.494 | 1.9361 | 0.9680 |
| 3 | 1424.208 | 2.7630 | 0.9210 |
| 4 | 1156.957 | 3.4012 | 0.8503 |
| 5 | 1084.882 | 3.6272 | 0.7254 |
| 6 | 1127.005 | 3.4916 | 0.5819 |
| 7 | 982.551 | 4.0049 | 0.5721 |
| 8 | 931.623 | 4.2239 | 0.5280 |

### Amdahl's Law

- Serial fraction: 0.017991 (1.7991%)
- Theoretical maximum speedup: 55.5847
- Theoretical speedup at 8 processes: 7.1052
- Observed speedup at 8 processes: 4.2239

The observed speedup is lower than the theoretical prediction. This difference can be explained by multiprocessing overhead, communication costs, and resource contention.

### Experiment 2B — Weak Scaling

| Processes | Problem Size | Time (ms) | Weak Efficiency | Gustafson Speedup |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 250,000 | 612.670 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 |
| 2 | 500,000 | 646.844 | 0.9472 | 1.9656 |
| 3 | 750,000 | 689.699 | 0.8883 | 2.9020 |
| 4 | 1,000,000 | 735.160 | 0.8334 | 3.8041 |
| 5 | 1,250,000 | 874.004 | 0.7010 | 4.7210 |
| 6 | 1,500,000 | 1011.994 | 0.6054 | 5.6332 |
| 7 | 1,750,000 | 1090.250 | 0.5620 | 6.5220 |
| 8 | 2,000,000 | 1207.391 | 0.5074 | 7.4121 |

### Analysis

Strong scaling improved as the number of processes increased. The best measured execution time was 931.623 ms with 8 processes, giving a speedup of 4.2239.

However, parallel efficiency decreased to 52.80% at 8 processes. The observed speedup was below the theoretical Amdahl prediction.

In weak scaling, the problem size increased proportionally with the number of processes. Execution time increased from 612.670 ms to 1207.391 ms, while weak efficiency decreased to 50.74%.

These results indicate that adding processes does not provide perfectly linear performance scaling. Serial work, multiprocessing overhead, and hardware resource limitations affect the results.

## Phase 4 — Load Balancing and Scheduling Strategies

### Experimental Setup

- Number of tasks: 12,000
- Number of processes: 8
- Task cost: K(i) = 1 + floor((i / 60)^2)
- Scheduling strategies: Static, Cyclic, Dynamic
- Dynamic chunk sizes: 1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024

### Experimental Results

Serial baseline time: 3106.636 ms.

| Strategy | Time (ms) | Speedup | Omega |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Static | 1159.050 | 2.6803 | 2.5025 |
| Cyclic | 707.772 | 4.3893 | 1.0280 |
| Dynamic-1 | 854.712 | 3.6347 | 1.0090 |
| Dynamic-4 | 729.083 | 4.2610 | 1.0034 |
| Dynamic-16 | 730.626 | 4.2520 | 1.0107 |
| Dynamic-64 | 735.992 | 4.2210 | 1.0301 |
| Dynamic-256 | 765.891 | 4.0562 | 1.1259 |
| Dynamic-1024 | 946.919 | 3.2808 | 1.6296 |

### Analysis

The cyclic scheduling strategy achieved the best execution time of 707.772 ms and a speedup of 4.3893.

Static block scheduling performed worst among the tested strategies, with an execution time of 1159.050 ms and a load imbalance ratio (Omega) of 2.5025. This indicates that consecutive blocks contained tasks with significantly different computational costs.

Cyclic scheduling distributed tasks more evenly among processes, achieving an Omega value of 1.0280.

Dynamic scheduling with chunk size 4 achieved the lowest load imbalance ratio of 1.0034. However, its execution time was slightly higher than cyclic scheduling due to scheduling overhead.

Large dynamic chunks, especially 1024, reduced scheduling overhead but increased load imbalance.

Overall, cyclic scheduling provided the best balance between execution time and workload distribution in this experiment.
